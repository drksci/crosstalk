# Literature study: delay lines, transversal filters, delay reservoirs and ternary networks

## Section A. The lineage: from mercury tanks to a ternary coax network

*Provenance rule for this file: every reference below was opened or looked up in a registry (Crossref, the arXiv API, or the publisher page) during this study; Section B states the source for each. Facts quoted from Wikipedia are marked "(secondary)". Where I describe something from general knowledge without a checked source, I say so.*

### 1. A memory that is a delay

The first idea in this story is that a signal in transit is storage. A pulse launched into a medium with a known propagation speed comes out later, and if you feed the output back into the input you have a memory that remembers as long as the amplifier stays on. von Neumann's 1945 EDVAC report names delay-line storage as the memory for the machine [vonneumann1945]; the engineering was done by Eckert and colleagues at the Moore School, whose paper on mercury lines at "a pulse rate of several megacycles" appeared in the Proceedings of the IRE in 1949 [eckert1949], and Eckert's own 1953 survey is a good contemporary view of the trade-offs among delay lines, CRT stores and drums [eckert1953].

The mercury tanks are the famous version. Mercury was chosen because its acoustic impedance is close to that of the quartz transducers, and it was held at 40 C so that match did not drift; sound in mercury travels at 1450 m/s (secondary: [wp_delayline]). EDSAC's lines each held 560 bits, 36 pulses per word with one spacer (secondary: [wp_delayline]). The Cambridge pages give the machine totals: 32 tanks, 1024 locations of 17 bits arranged as two batteries of 512 words (the second running from 1952), a 500 kHz pulse rate, a 1.5 ms average order time, about 3000 valves and about 12 kW [cam_edsac]; the first programs ran on 6 May 1949 (secondary: [wp_edsac]). The designers' own accounts are [wilkes1950]. UNIVAC I stored 1000 words of 12 characters in 126 mercury channels in seven tanks, clocked at 2.25 MHz (secondary: [wp_univac]).

The cheaper cousin was the magnetostrictive wire: torsional pulses in coiled steel wire, with nickel pieces as transducers. A board of about a square foot held on the order of a kilobit with access times around 500 microseconds (secondary: [wp_delayline]); it ran the Friden EC-130 calculator, introduced in June 1963 per its own article (the delay-line article says 1964; secondary: [wp_friden]), and it stored the screen of early terminals, one line of four rows of 40 six-bit characters (secondary: [wp_delayline]). For contrast, the Williams-Kilburn CRT store gave random access to any bit at the cost of tube-tuning misery [williams1949].

Why did delay-line memory fade? The honest answer from the pages I opened is only the structural one: the memory is serial, so you wait for the bit to come round (UNIVAC I averaged about 222 microseconds, secondary: [wp_delayline]), and mercury needs a thermostat and is poisonous. The common story that random-access core, and later chips, simply replaced them is plausible but I did not tie it to a single opened source. The relevant point for this project is the other direction: as a memory the delay line was a liability, but as a *computing* element, a thing that presents a window of the recent past at fixed taps, it is exactly what a convolution wants.

### 2. The transversal filter: delay, weight, sum

In 1940 Kallmann published "Transversal Filters" in the Proceedings of the IRE [kallmann1940]: a tapped delay line, a weight on each tap, and a summing junction. Everything in this project is a descendant. What changed over the decades was the physics of the delay.

*Charge as the delay.* Sangster and Teer at Philips described the bucket-brigade device in 1969, a chain of MOS switches and capacitors that hands packets of charge along under two clocks [sangster1969]. A year later Boyle and Smith at Bell Labs published the charge-coupled device, the same idea with the gates abutting [boyle1970]. Both are analog shift registers, and by 1972 the CCD was being used as a tapped delay line to build analog matched filters [collins1972]. The bucket brigade survives as the audio BBD: Panasonic's MN3007 is a 1024-stage part, the MN3005 has 4096 (secondary: [wp_bbd]), and a 2024 Hackaday piece explains the bucket-brigade idea well [hd_bbd2024].

*Sound on a crystal as the delay.* White and Voltmer showed in 1965 that interdigital electrodes can launch and receive surface acoustic waves on a piezoelectric substrate [white1965]; Tancrell and Holland (1971) explain how placing the electrode taps makes a transversal filter [tancrell1971]; Quate and Thompson (1970) showed a nonlinear acoustic interaction that computes convolution and correlation in real time [quate1970]; and Maines and Paige (1976) review SAW filters, delay lines and convolvers for signal processing [maines1976]. These are the closest hardware cousins of a passive tapped coax: analog, passive, weights set by geometry.

*Radar's matched filter.* Cook's 1960 paper on pulse compression [cook1960] and North's matched-filter analysis (the Proceedings reprint is 1963) [north1963] are the classical reason anyone built long tapped delay lines at RF: a filter whose impulse response is the time-reverse of the expected waveform maximises output signal-to-noise. Notice what that is: a hand-designed, one-layer, fixed-weight classifier for exactly one template. A learned stack of such filters is a neural network.

### 3. Neural precursors: weights learned, delays kept

Widrow and Hoff's 1960 "Adaptive Switching Circuits" is ADALINE and the LMS rule [widrow1960]; Widrow and Lehr's later survey connects the adaptive filter to neural networks [widrow1990]. The adaptive transversal filter is a tapped delay line with learned analog weights, which is precisely the structure here with ternary weights.

Time-delay neural networks put the delays inside the network: Waibel et al. in 1989 fed delayed copies of the input to weighted units for phoneme recognition [waibel1989], a convolution in all but name. The analog-hardware branch of this idea matters more for us. Tank and Hopfield's 1987 PNAS paper builds an analog network in which delays are arranged so that a sequence of inputs is "concentrated in time" into one network state [tank1987], and Unnikrishnan, Hopfield and Tank applied time-delayed connections to connected-digit recognition [unnikrishnan1991]. (I confirmed these titles and venues in the DOI registry; I did not read the full texts, so I do not claim how far toward circuits they went.)

The modern descendants are dilated causal convolutions: Yu and Koltun's dilated convolutions [yu2015], WaveNet's stack with growing dilations [wavenet2016], and Bai et al.'s TCN, which showed the same structure is a competitive generic sequence model [bai2018]. The dilations 1, 4, 16 in this project are WaveNet-style receptive-field growth, with the twist that a dilation is just a longer cable between taps.

### 4. Delay as a reservoir

A separate strand uses a delay line not as the filter you train but as the memory of a reservoir. Appeltant et al. (2011) showed one nonlinear node plus a delay loop, time-multiplexed into "virtual nodes", can do reservoir computing [appeltant2011]. Paquot et al. [paquot2012] and Larger et al. [larger2012] built optoelectronic versions; Brunner et al. pushed an all-photonic one to gigabyte-per-second rates [brunner2013]; Ortín et al. unified the delay reservoir with extreme learning machines [ortin2015]. Appeltant et al. (2014) studied binary input masks for these systems [appeltant2014], an early sighting of restricted-alphabet weights in a delay-based machine. Digital realisations exist, such as the FPGA implementation of Alomar et al. [alomar2015]; reviews are [vandersande2017] (photonic) and [tanaka2019] (physical reservoirs generally). One group applied a delay-based photonic reservoir to modulation-format identification in fibre links [cai2020].

What the reservoir strand teaches: a nonlinear node plus a delay is a legitimate computer, and a *fixed* random system with a trained readout is a respectable design. This project differs in the standard way: it is feed-forward, the delays are trained *structure* (which taps exist and with what sign), and only the final readout is digital. It borrows the idea of a single analog nonlinearity and a digital readout.

### 5. Analog and in-memory neural hardware

Mead's 1990 "Neuromorphic electronic systems" is the manifesto for doing neural computation in analog silicon [mead1990]. Intel's ETANN (10240 floating-gate synapses) was the commercial analog neural chip of 1989 [holler1989]; Tsividis and Anastassiou described switched-capacitor neural networks in 1987 [tsividis1987]; and Agranat et al. built a neural processor on CCD charge transfer with 65536 programmable analog synapses [agranat1990]. Today's analog AI includes IBM's phase-change-memory work [ambrogio2018], photonic meshes [shen2017] and Lightmatter's 2025 Nature paper on a photonic processor running ResNet and BERT [ahmed2025]. A small but growing line of work computes in the time domain, where values are pulse widths or delays; a 2024 ISQED comparison notes it has received little research attention and is most efficient for small and medium arrays [freye2024].

I looked for a specific citation to support the claim that "a differential-pair tanh is standard in neuromorphic circuits" and did not source one; the BJT-pair tanh is textbook analog design, and the project uses it as such. I make no literature claim about it.

### 6. Ternary weights and hardware-aware training

BinaryConnect [courbariaux2015], Binarized Neural Networks [courbariaux2016] and XNOR-Net [rastegari2016] established that weights can be reduced to one bit; Ternary Weight Networks [li2016] and Trained Ternary Quantization [zhu2016] added the zero. The straight-through estimator [bengio2013] is the trick that makes training through the rounding possible. BitNet [wang2023] and BitNet b1.58 [ma2024] brought the ternary alphabet {-1, 0, +1} with absmean scaling to language models, which is where this project borrows its quantisation rule. For analog robustness, injecting noise into weights during training goes back to Murray and Edwards [murray1994], and modern analog-in-memory work packages the idea as hardware-aware training [rasch2023].

### 7. The task: modulation recognition, and the honest context

O'Shea, Corgan and Clancy applied CNNs to modulation recognition [oshea2016]; the datasets were described by O'Shea and West [osheawest2016], and the over-the-air extension and RadioML 2018.01A by O'Shea, Roy and Clancy [oshea2018]. DeepSig states that all its datasets are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (non-commercial, share-alike) and describes them as unsupported with known errata [deepsig]; this project does not use them, since it generates its own synthetic data. Classical feature-based AMC is represented by Swami and Sadler's cumulant classifier [swami2000].

For calibration: a 2025 lightweight model reports *average* accuracies of 61.20%, 63.78% and 62.13% on RML2016.10a, 2016.10b and 2018.01a [suman2025]. Those averages include low-SNR examples where accuracy is limited. This project's 86-92% (ternary V 0.863, float 0.919) is on its own synthetic 8-class set with SNR from 0 to 20 dB. Different class sets, different SNR ranges, different generator: **the numbers are not comparable and must not be presented as a result on any public benchmark.** Restricting SNR to 0-20 dB removes the hardest low-SNR examples that pull public-benchmark averages down, so a higher number is expected and is not evidence of a better method.

### 8. Builders, then us

On the hobby side, the real, checked links are: a delay-line memory in a glass plate [hd_glass2012], bucket-brigade explainers [hd_bbd2024], the open-source analog computer The Analog Thing [that_site], and Bowyer's modular op-amp analog neural net [bowyer2024], which uses comparator thresholds, not tanh, and no delay lines (from the page). I did not find a hobbyist ternary tapped-delay-line network.

The closest work I found is on the RF side: Pour and Ha's ISCAS 2025 paper on RF-domain recurrent networks, and a 2026 Virginia Tech master's thesis that says the original RF-domain RNN uses a passive tapped transmission-line delay and replaces it with an active gm-C all-pass delay line [pour2025][merton2026]. Add Woo et al.'s ternary-weight modulation-recognition hardware [woo2024] and the digital ancestry above, and the pieces of this project are all present in the literature. What this project does is put a *feed-forward ternary dilated convolutional stack* onto lengths of cable and couplers on paper, evaluate the same trained weights at four fidelities, and report the gap between the clean math and the lossy cable honestly. That is an integration and a stress-test, not a new principle.

## Section C. What is genuinely new here, and what is not

**Not new (all of these have prior work in Section B):**

1. *A tapped delay line computing a weighted sum.* This is the transversal filter of 1940 [kallmann1940], built in CCD, BBD and SAW form and used as an analog matched filter [collins1972][tancrell1971][maines1976].
2. *Delay lines inside neural networks.* TDNNs [waibel1989], and analog networks whose delays concentrate a sequence in time [tank1987][unnikrishnan1991].
3. *Delay-based analog computing for learning.* Delay-loop reservoirs, in electronic and photonic form, including FPGA versions, binary input masks and a modulation-format task [appeltant2011][paquot2012][larger2012][appeltant2014][alomar2015][cai2020].
4. *Dilated causal convolution stacks* [wavenet2016][bai2018][yu2015].
5. *Ternary weights with the BitNet b1.58 rule and straight-through training* [ma2024][li2016][zhu2016][bengio2013].
6. *Analog neural hardware with analog nonlinearities and noise-aware training* [mead1990][holler1989][tsividis1987][agranat1990][murray1994][rasch2023]; time-domain compute-in-memory [freye2024].
7. *Ternary-weight hardware for modulation recognition* in the digital domain [woo2024].

**Closest prior work that could undercut a novelty claim (read this before writing "first"):**

- **RF-domain recurrent neural networks with a tapped transmission-line delay** [pour2025][merton2026]. A Virginia Tech thesis describes the original RF-domain RNN as relying on "a passive tapped transmission line delay for sequential input samples" and proposes an active gm-C delay line instead. Their target is anomaly detection in wireless systems, not modulation recognition, and the network is recurrent. I read only the thesis abstract page and found the ISCAS paper by DOI; I did not read either full text, so I cannot say how close their circuit is (for example whether they use ternary or signed taps, or a tanh stage). Treat "first analog delay-line neural network at RF" as **unsupportable**.
- **Tank and Hopfield 1987 and Unnikrishnan, Hopfield and Tank 1991** [tank1987][unnikrishnan1991]: analog delay-based networks for sequence recognition, 35 years earlier. I did not read them in full.
- **Woo et al. 2024 (IEEE Access)** [woo2024] and the RFSoC ternary AMC work found in search: ternary weights plus modulation recognition is already published, in digital hardware.

**What is plausibly distinctive, stated modestly:**

1. *The specific mapping:* a feed-forward, three-layer, ternary dilated-convolution network whose weights are tap present/absent/inverted (coupler, no coupler, 180 degree hybrid) on lengths of cable with dilations 1, 4, 16. I found no paper that states this mapping, but my search was a handful of web queries, not a systematic review; "I did not find" is the strongest honest statement. Signed taps themselves are old in transversal filters (from general knowledge, not checked against a source here).
2. *Same weights, four fidelities* (ideal math, fixed-point with RTL cross-check, SPICE, RF channel), with the gap between them reported. This is a method contribution to reproducibility, not a physics contribution.
3. *Honest negative result:* the C and R tiers lose 16-20 points to cable loss and layer-3 saturation at 32 dB/us (project `README.md`). Showing where such a design breaks is useful and is cheap to say.

**What the project does not claim and the literature does not support:** that a ternary delay-line network beats digital for modulation recognition; that results on synthetic data transfer to real channels; that the numbers compare to RadioML benchmarks [suman2025]; that any hardware exists.

**Licensing note:** DeepSig states that all its datasets are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 [deepsig]. The project generates its own synthetic data and does not redistribute RadioML.

**Unverified or dropped items (not cited above):**
- Jaeger 2001 echo-state report (GMD technical report): no registry record checked, so not listed.
- Mythic and the Intel "80170" mapping to the ETANN paper: the ETANN IJCNN paper is verified, but the 80170 part number is from memory. Mythic: only a 2022 IEDM talk record by a single author was seen; its affiliation was not verified, so it is dropped.
- A CCD/transversal-filter paper by Baertsch et al. (1976) and Wilkes's "Computers Then and Now" were not found in Crossref; dropped.
- Tridgell et al., "Real-time Automatic Modulation Classification using RFSoC" (PDF opened, title and authors read; venue not stated, so dropped from the table).
- Hackaday.io memristor-simulator and EDSAC-replica items surfaced in search only; not opened, dropped. A Hackaday article about a BBD-based or delay-line-based neural net does not appear to exist in my search results.
- The original source for the 51.2 ms MN3007 delay figure: seen only in a forum search snippet, not used.
